# Tower Defense Wave Generation and Enemy Scheduling

## The Essence in One Sentence

On the surface, it's about configuring monster counts and spawn order; in reality, it uses "budget scalars" to unify difficulty measurement and "beat segmentation" to control the rhythm of instantaneous pressure. This article focuses on explaining how to decouple these two using a structured DSL, achieving a predictable pressure curve with strategic depth.

The "fun/frustration" of Tower Defense doesn't just come from numerical strength, but from whether the **pressure curve is controllable**:

- Early game: Let players understand the loop of "Build → Successfully Defend → Earn Resources"
- Mid game: Let players make choices (which to upgrade first? What counter to switch to?)
- Late game: Create predictable peaks and valleys, forming a narrative sense of "Wave N is coming"

This article offers an actionable solution: use **Wave DSL (structured configuration) + Scheduler** to generate monsters, and use "Budget + Beat" to make pressure a controllable variable.

---

## 1. A Wave is Not "Spawning X Monsters," It's a Rhythm Segment

It is recommended to break a level into rhythm segments:

- Warmup: A small number of low-threat enemies, allowing players to build and trial-and-error
- Ramp: Gradually increasing pressure (quantity/speed/special enemies)
- Peak: A challenging wave (but it must be explainable: players know why they lost)
- Breather: A reward and adjustment window (upgrade/repair/choose skills)

When writing your wave configuration, you must be able to express these segments, rather than just writing `wave = 20 enemies`.

---

## 2. Budget: Unified Management of "How Hard is This Wave"

Use budget management to control the intensity of each wave, with the core being mapping "difficulty" to a comparable scalar:

`budget = base + growth(waveIndex)`

Each enemy has a cost:

- Minion: cost 1
- Elite: cost 4
- Heavy Armor: cost 6
- Flying: cost 5 (because it forces players to switch towers/strategies)

Then this wave becomes "selecting monsters within the budget":

- Allows easy variation: swapping templates under the same budget (more minions vs. fewer elites)
- Allows stable pressure control: increasing the budget makes it harder, without manually tuning every parameter

---

## 3. Beat: Solving "How to Spawn Within the Same Wave"

Player experience pressure depends not only on the total amount but also on the spawn rhythm:

- Spawning all at once: High instantaneous pressure, easily leading to instant collapse or instant clear
- Spawning in batches: Feels more like "breathing," allowing players to micro-adjust while defending

A simple and effective approach is to break a wave into several segments, each with a beat:

- `segment.durationMs`
- `segment.spawnEveryMs`
- `segment.packSize` (how many spawn at once)
- `segment.pool` (optional enemy pool)

When tuning parameters, you can first adjust only `spawnEveryMs` instead of changing quantities everywhere.

---

## 4. Wave DSL (Example Structure)

You don't need to build a full language immediately; using JSON/TS configuration works too. The key is structured expression:

- Which rhythm segment this wave belongs to (warmup/ramp/peak/breather)
- What the budget for this wave is
- What segments this wave has (how the beat progresses)
- Which variants are allowed (Flying/Heavy Armor/Self-Destruct/Healer)

The benefit of writing a DSL is that you can build tools (visual preview/replay/automatic balancing).

---

## 5. Enemy "Variants" Are Designed to Create Recognition and Counters

The appeal of Tower Defense lies in players seeing the monster's form and knowing "how to respond."

Therefore, variant design should revolve around "forcing a strategy change":

- Flying: Bypasses ground towers → Forces building anti-air
- Heavy Armor: High HP/Damage Reduction → Forces high DPS or armor-piercing
- Self-Destruct: Explodes on proximity → Forces crowd control/slowing/concentrated fire
- Healer: Extends the front line → Forces point-and-click killing or interrupting

If variants are just "reskin + higher numbers," players won't feel they are executing strategy.

---

## 6. Difficulty Growth: Use Curves Instead of Guessing

Common growth curves:

- Linear: `budget = a + b * wave`
- Exponential: `budget = a + b * wave^p` (p>1 leads to explosive growth in late game)
- Segmented: Fast ramp early, plateau mid, then another sprint late (better experience)

Engineering-wise, it is recommended to start with segmented:

- wave 1~5: Fast growth (teach the player)
- 6~15: Slow growth (give strategy space)
- 16+: Short, sharp sprints (create the climax)

---

## 7. Acceptance Checklist: Treat the "Wave System" as a Testable Module

Minimum acceptance criteria:

- [ ] With the same seed and configuration, wave generation results are reproducible (facilitates debugging)
- [ ] Budget stays within controllable range (no "peak wave suddenly doubles")
- [ ] Variant appearance frequency is controllable (no 5 consecutive waves of Flying)
- [ ] Breather waves provide enough "upgrade window" (don't make the monster-free gap too short)

---

## 8. Next Steps (Combined with Demo/Tools)

Once you've structured the wave into a DSL, you can naturally build:

- Preview tool: Graph each wave's budget/beat
- Simulator: Quickly run 100 games to check P50/P90 clear waves
- Editor: Designers can adjust the rhythm without changing code

## Boundary Conditions

- Must handle the warmup segment to let players complete the initial loop, avoiding instant death at start.
- Must handle ramp segment budget growing exponentially with wave index to prevent late-game numerical collapse.
- Must handle peak segment alternating peaks and valleys to ensure players have explainable reasons for failure.
- Must handle breather segment providing sufficient upgrade or repair windows to avoid resource gaps.
- Must handle variant frequency distribution to prevent a single strategy failing across multiple consecutive waves.

## Design Trade-offs

- **Benefit**: Budget uniformly manages difficulty scalars; **Cost**: Designers must pre-define fixed costs for each monster type.
- **Benefit**: Beat segmentation makes pressure controllable like breathing; **Cost**: Scheduling logic is more complex than spawning all at once.
- **Benefit**: Variants force players to switch strategies (e.g., anti-air/armor-piercing); **Cost**: Increases maintenance cost of the enemy pool.
- **Benefit**: Segmented difficulty curve improves mid-game experience; **Cost**: Engineering requires manual division of wave intervals.
