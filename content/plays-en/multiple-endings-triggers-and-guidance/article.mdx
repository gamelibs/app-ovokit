# Multi-Endings: Trigger Conditions and Player Path Guidance

## The One-Sentence Essence

On the surface, it lets players experience different story directions; actually, the core mechanism dynamically determines trigger conditions through variable combinations and a centralized evaluator. This article focuses on how to design interpretable trigger logic, tiered hints, and path guidance that reduces replay costs.

Traffic Reason: High discussion rate; players will ask, "Which ending did you get?" — it naturally drives sharing and replay motivation.

## 1) Ending Conditions: Make Them "Explainable," Not Just "Purely Hidden"

It is recommended to split ending conditions into three parts:

- Main story progress (mandatory)  
- Key choices (branches)  
- Collection/Achievement thresholds (bonus)

Bottom line: At least guarantee that **playing normally** can reach 1~2 endings; otherwise, most players will feel the "content is incomplete."

## 2) Hint Intensity Must Be Tiered

Different players have very different tolerance for hints, so do not choose just one hint method.

- **Weak hint**: Environmental clues/line dialogue hints (for hardcore players)  
- **Medium hint**: Log/Compendium progress (for mass-market players)  
- **Strong hint**: Explicit "You will miss a certain route" warning (protects immersion)

Experience: A strong hint does not equal spoilers; it is protecting the "sense of investment."

## 3) Path Guidance: Give Backtracking Options, Not a Full Restart

The key to doing multi-endings well is: a second playthrough should not be repetitive labor.

You can offer:

- Chapter backtracking/key save points  
- Differentiated level mechanics for different endings (at least change one mechanic)  
- Ending rewards feeding back into the next playthrough (drives the loop)

One-sentence summary: **The value of multi-endings is "discussion and replay," provided "players know how to proceed."**

---

## 4) How to Implement "Explainability" for Ending Conditions

A practical approach is to split ending conditions into three types of variables:

- **Choice variables**: Key options (A/B/C), determining the main branch
- **Threshold variables**: Collection/Favorability/Completion (numerical thresholds)
- **Flag variables**: One-time events (whether someone was saved, whether something was destroyed)

Then, use "Progress Hints" within the game to make the presence of these variables visible:

- Log/Compendium shows the percentage of "Ending Clues"
- At the end of a chapter, give a weak hint like "You are getting closer to one direction"

This way, players won't feel that endings just "pop up suddenly."

---

## 5) Guidance and Error Prevention: Let Players Avoid "Irreversible" Pitfalls

The most common point of negative reviews for multi-endings is:

- Players invest a lot of time, only to realize they took the wrong route and must restart

Common protection strategies:

- Auto-save at key nodes (chapter save points)
- Give strong hints at irreversible nodes ("Continuing will lock you into a certain route")
- Provide a backtracking mechanism (return to the nearest key node, not a full restart)

This is not about lowering difficulty; it is about protecting the sense of investment.

---

## 6) Engineering Implementation Advice: Use a "Centralized Ending Evaluator"

Do not write `if` statements scattered throughout the story script to judge endings; instead, centralize them in an evaluator:

- Input: Set of variables (flags + counters + choices)
- Output: Ending ID + Trigger reason (for UI explanation)

This allows you to:

- Unit test coverage for every ending
- Print "Why this ending was triggered" during debugging
- Avoid breaking old logic when adding new endings later

## Boundary Conditions

- Must guarantee that the normal playthrough can reach at least 1~2 endings; otherwise, it will be considered "incomplete content."
- Must handle situations where players cannot unlock specific endings due to missing key choices or collectibles.
- Must provide strong hints or auto-saves at irreversible key nodes to prevent players from falling into dead ends due to accidental operations.
- Must handle conflicts in multi-ending judgment logic to ensure mutually exclusive endings are not triggered simultaneously in a single playthrough.
- Must handle situations where adding new endings does not break existing logic and unit test coverage.

## Design Trade-offs

- **Benefit**: Logical reuse and easier debugging via a centralized evaluator, **Cost**: Requires developing an independent evaluation module, **Alternative**: Writing numerous `if` judgments scattered in story scripts.
- **Benefit**: Provides chapter backtracking or differentiated levels to reduce repetitive labor in a second playthrough, **Cost**: Requires designing additional level mechanics or save management logic, **Alternative**: Requires players to completely restart the game from the beginning.
- **Benefit**: Uses strong hints to protect player investment and clearly guide direction, **Cost**: May weaken the sense of exploration achievement for hardcore players, **Alternative**: Relies solely on environmental or dialogue hints.
